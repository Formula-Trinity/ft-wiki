    Cooling System¶

    What is a Cooling System and why do we need it?¶

    An internal combustion engine produces a large amount of heat during operation. Only a portion of the chemical energy released from the fuel is converted into useful mechanical work at the crankshaft, while the rest is lost as heat through the exhaust gases, cylinder walls, piston crown, cylinder head, valves, oil, and surrounding engine structure. Because of this, the engine requires a dedicated cooling system to control its temperature and prevent thermal damage.[1]

    Figure 1: Work done by heat engine (Otto Engine) compared to heat released.

    The cooling system is normally a liquid-based circuit made up of a radiator, water pump, thermostat, coolant passages, hoses, pressure cap, expansion tank, and cooling fan. Coolant is circulated through the engine block and cylinder head, where it absorbs heat from the metal surfaces around the combustion chamber. This heated coolant is then carried to the radiator, where the heat is transferred to the surrounding air. The water pump keeps the coolant moving through the system, while the thermostat controls when coolant is allowed to flow through the radiator.[1][2]

    Figure 2: Diagram of a Cooling System

    The cooling system is not simply used to make the engine as cold as possible. In fact, an engine works best when it reaches and maintains a controlled operating temperature. When the engine is too cold, fuel does not vaporise as effectively, frictional losses are higher, oil circulation is less efficient, and emissions increase. For this reason, the thermostat initially restricts coolant flow to the radiator, allowing the engine to warm up quickly. Once the coolant approaches the correct operating temperature, the thermostat opens and allows coolant to pass through the radiator, maintaining a stable temperature during driving.[1][2]

    The cooling system is therefore needed for three main engineering reasons. Firstly, it prevents overheating. Combustion temperatures are extremely high, and without heat removal the cylinder head, block, pistons, valves, and gaskets would be exposed to excessive thermal stress. This can lead to warped cylinder heads, cracked engine blocks, piston scuffing, failed head gaskets, and in severe cases complete engine seizure. If heat is not removed from the engine, the piston can expand excessively and lose its running clearance inside the cylinder, causing major mechanical failure.[1][2]

    Figure 3: Cooling system removing heat from an engine.

    Secondly, the cooling system helps maintain correct lubrication. Engine oil must remain within a suitable temperature range so that it can maintain its viscosity and form a protective film between moving components. If the oil becomes too hot, it thins out and breaks down more quickly, reducing its ability to protect bearings, piston rings, camshafts, and cylinder walls. By controlling engine temperature, the cooling system indirectly protects the lubrication system and reduces mechanical wear.[1][2]

    Thirdly, the cooling system improves performance, efficiency, and emissions. A controlled operating temperature allows the engine management system to run more efficient fuelling and ignition strategies. If the engine remains too cold, it may require richer fuelling and may produce higher emissions. If it becomes too hot, the engine may suffer from knock, reduced power, oil degradation, and possible component damage. The cooling system therefore keeps the engine within a narrow thermal window where combustion, lubrication, and mechanical reliability are all balanced.[1][2]

    Parts of a Cooling System¶

    A cooling system is made up of several components that work together to remove heat from the engine and maintain a stable operating temperature. In a water-cooled internal combustion engine, the coolant circulates through the engine block and cylinder head, absorbs heat from the combustion areas, and then releases this heat to the surrounding air through the radiator. For Bruce, which uses a Honda CBR600RR engine, these components must be packaged into the Formula Student chassis while still providing reliable cooling, safe fluid containment, and compliance with FSUK regulations.[1][2]

    Radiator¶

    Figure 4: Radiator

    The radiator is the main heat exchanger in the cooling system. Its purpose is to transfer heat from the hot coolant to the surrounding air. Hot coolant enters the radiator from the engine and passes through a series of small internal tubes. Thin fins are attached to these tubes to increase the surface area available for heat transfer. As air flows through the radiator core, heat is removed from the coolant and rejected to the atmosphere.[3]

    Radiators are commonly made from aluminium because it is lightweight, corrosion-resistant, and has good thermal conductivity. This makes it suitable for a Formula Student car where mass, packaging, and heat transfer performance are all important. The radiator usually contains an inlet, outlet, core, fins, mounting points, and sometimes a drain plug or filler neck depending on the design.[3]

    For Bruce, the radiator is positioned within the sidepod so that it can receive airflow while remaining protected inside the car packaging. This is different from the original motorcycle installation, where the radiator is mounted directly in front of the engine and receives unobstructed airflow.[3]

    Water Pump¶

    Figure 5: Water Pump

    The water pump is responsible for circulating coolant around the cooling system. It moves coolant from the radiator back into the engine block and cylinder head, allowing the cycle of heat absorption and heat rejection to continue. Without the water pump, coolant would not circulate effectively, and the engine would quickly develop local hot spots.[4]

    In operation, cooled water leaves the radiator and is pushed by the water pump into the engine. It then flows through coolant passages around the cylinders and combustion chamber areas, where it absorbs heat. The heated coolant then returns to the radiator to be cooled again. This continuous circulation allows the engine temperature to remain controlled during operation.[4]

    On the CBR600RR engine, the standard water pump is designed for the original motorcycle cooling system. When used in Bruce, the pump must be able to overcome the resistance of the new radiator, hoses, bends, fittings, and any additional pipe length introduced by the chassis layout. Poor hose routing, excessive restrictions, or trapped air pockets could reduce coolant flow and affect cooling performance.

    Thermostat¶

    Figure 6: Thermostat

    The thermostat is a temperature-controlled valve that regulates coolant flow through the radiator. When the engine is cold, the thermostat remains closed or partially closed, preventing coolant from flowing fully through the radiator. This allows the engine to warm up more quickly. Once the coolant reaches the thermostat opening temperature, the valve opens and allows coolant to pass through the radiator.[5]

    Most automotive thermostats use a wax element. As the coolant temperature increases, the wax expands and pushes the valve open. As the coolant temperature decreases, the wax contracts and the valve closes again. This allows the cooling system to maintain the engine close to its intended operating temperature.[5]

    Figure 7: Thermostat closed vs Thermostat Open

    The thermostat is important because an engine should not be run too cold or too hot. If the engine is too cold, fuel vaporisation is poorer, friction is higher, oil flow may be less effective, and emissions increase. If the engine is too hot, the oil can thin out, parts can expand excessively, and the engine may suffer from knock, gasket failure, or mechanical damage.[5]

    For Bruce, the thermostat helps the Honda CBR600RR engine reach its working temperature efficiently before maintaining a controlled temperature during running. If the thermostat is removed or modified, the warm-up behaviour, coolant flow balance, and temperature stability of the engine could be worse.

    Hoses and Pipework¶

    Figure 8: Hoses and pipework

    The hoses and pipework connect the engine, radiator, water pump, thermostat housing, and expansion or catch system. They provide the path through which the coolant flows around the system. In most vehicles, flexible rubber hoses are used where movement or vibration is expected, while aluminium or steel hard pipes may be used for longer or more rigid sections.[6]

    For a FS car, hose routing is especially important. The hoses must avoid sharp bends, rubbing points, hot exhaust areas, moving components, and areas where they could be damaged during maintenance or operation. The hoses must also be securely clamped and supported so that vibration does not cause leaks or fatigue.[6]

    Since Bruce uses plain water as the coolant, any leak would reduce cooling performance and could create a safety issue on track. Therefore, hose quality, clamp selection, pipe bends, and proper routing are essential parts of the cooling system design.

    Expansion tank, overflow tank, and catch can.¶

    Figure 9: Catch can for our car Bruce

    As the coolant heats up, it expands. The cooling system therefore requires a way to manage this expansion safely. In many vehicles, an expansion or overflow tank is connected to the radiator or pressure cap. This allows excess coolant to move into the tank as the system heats up and, in some systems, return to the cooling circuit as the engine cools down.[7]

    For FSUK, the cooling system also requires a catch can arrangement so that any vented coolant is collected safely rather than released onto the track. This is especially important for Bruce because the car uses a water-based cooling system and must comply with FSUK fluid containment rules. The catch can must be sized and mounted correctly so that any coolant expelled from the system is contained.[7]

    The expansion or catch system also helps prevent air from remaining trapped in the cooling circuit. Air pockets reduce heat transfer and can cause local overheating, particularly in the cylinder head. For this reason, the filler point, bleed points, and tank position should be considered during the design of the cooling layout.[7

    Pressure Cap¶

    Figure 10: Pressure Cap

    The pressure cap controls the pressure inside the cooling system. By allowing the system to operate above atmospheric pressure, the boiling point of the coolant is increased. This helps prevent boiling in hot areas of the engine, especially around the cylinder head and exhaust valve regions.[8]

    If the pressure becomes too high, the pressure cap opens and allows coolant or vapour to escape into the overflow or catch system. This protects the radiator, hoses, and engine from excessive pressure. However, if the pressure cap is faulty or incorrectly rated, the system may either over-pressurise or boil too easily.[8]

    Figure 11: Position of cap with increased pressure

    For Bruce, the pressure cap was selected to suit the radiator, hose, and engine requirements. It must also work correctly with the catch can or overflow system so that any released coolant is safely contained.

    Cooling Fan¶

    Figure 12: Cooling Fan

    The cooling fan helps draw or push air through the radiator when the car is moving slowly or stationary. At higher vehicle speeds, natural airflow through the radiator may be enough to remove heat. However, during low-speed running, pit operation, staging, or after a dynamic event, airflow may be too low for the radiator to work effectively on its own.[9]

    This is particularly relevant for a FS car because the vehicle often operates at relatively low speeds compared to a motorcycle or road car. The fan therefore provides an important backup source of airflow and helps prevent overheating when the car is not moving quickly enough to force air through the radiator.[9]

    Figure 13: How the fan pulls the air through the radiator

    For Bruce, the fan is positioned so that it improves airflow through the radiator core rather than allowing air to bypass it. The fan mounting, shroud design, electrical control, and temperature switch or ECU control strategy were considered as part of the cooling system design.

    Engine Cooling Passages¶

    Figure 14: Cooling Passages

    The coolant passages are internal channels cast or machined into the engine block and cylinder head. These passages allow coolant to flow around the hottest parts of the engine, especially near the cylinders, combustion chambers, exhaust ports, and valve seats. Their purpose is to absorb heat from the engine structure and carry it away to the radiator.

    Although these passages are part of the engine itself rather than external cooling hardware, they are essential to the operation of the system. Any blockage, air pocket, or poor flow distribution inside the engine can lead to local overheating, even if the radiator and pump are functioning correctly.

    Figure 15: All green areas are the cooling passages.

    In the Honda CBR600RR engine used in Bruce, the original coolant passages remain part of the engine design. The external cooling system must therefore provide suitable flow, pressure, and temperature control so that these internal passages can remove heat effectively.

    FSUK cooling rules and safety requirements¶

    The cooling system for Bruce must comply with Formula Student UK rules and the general safety requirements for an internal combustion vehicle. Since the car uses a water-cooled Honda CBR600RR engine, the cooling system is classed as a liquid cooling system and must use plain water as the coolant. Glycol-based antifreeze, coolant additives, or other slippery fluids are not permitted, as any leakage onto the track could reduce tyre grip and create a hazard for the driver and other vehicles. As a result, the system must be designed to operate reliably using water only, with suitable radiator capacity, airflow, pressure control, hose routing, and sealing.[10]

    Although water is safer than glycol-based coolant from a track-contamination point of view, it still presents several risks. During operation, the coolant becomes hot and pressurised. A failure of a hose, clamp, radiator joint, filler neck, or pressure cap could release hot water or steam, creating a burn hazard and causing rapid loss of cooling performance. Coolant loss could also reduce system pressure, lower the boiling margin, introduce air into the circuit, and cause local boiling inside the cylinder head or engine block. Therefore, all cooling system components must be securely mounted, correctly rated for temperature and pressure, and routed away from sharp edges, moving parts, electrical components, and hot exhaust surfaces.[10]

    The FSUK rules also require a dedicated catch can arrangement. Separate catch cans must be used for the engine coolant system and the engine lubrication system. The coolant catch can must contain either 10% of the total cooling system fluid volume or 900 ml, whichever value is greater. This ensures that if the cooling system vents through the pressure cap or overflow line, the expelled water is collected safely rather than released onto the track. For Bruce, the catch can is therefore part of the safety system rather than only an auxiliary component.[10]

    The catch can must be rigidly mounted so that it cannot become loose during vehicle operation, vibration, or impact. It must be positioned behind the firewall and below the driver’s shoulder level. The vent line must be routed down to the bottom level of the chassis and must exit outside the bodywork. This reduces the risk of hot fluid or vapour being directed towards the driver and ensures that any overflow is managed in a controlled and inspectable way.[10]

    The firewall requirements are also relevant to the cooling system layout. Any part of the liquid cooling system near the driver must be separated from the cockpit by a suitable firewall, as the system contains hot pressurised water. The radiator, hoses, filler neck, pressure cap, overflow line, and catch can must therefore be positioned with driver protection, inspection access, and scrutineering requirements in mind.[10]

    Material selection is another important safety consideration. Hoses, hard pipes, fittings, clamps, radiator mounts, and catch-can components must be suitable for the expected temperatures, pressures, vibration, and mechanical loads. Low-temperature plastics, weak hose materials, poor-quality clamps, or unsupported pipework should be avoided. Flexible hoses should be used where engine movement or vibration is expected, while longer pipe runs should be properly supported to prevent rubbing, fatigue, or accidental contact with surrounding components.[10]

    The cooling system must also be designed to avoid air locks and local hot spots. The filler point, bleed points, radiator position, hose routing, and catch-can connection should allow the system to be filled and bled properly. Trapped air reduces heat transfer and can cause local overheating, especially around the cylinder head and exhaust valve regions.

    For Bruce, these FSUK requirements define the minimum acceptable cooling system design. The final system must use plain water, contain vented fluid safely, protect the driver from hot pressurised coolant, prevent leakage onto the track, and remain reliable under vibration, heat, and dynamic vehicle loads. Passing scrutineering is the first requirement, but the cooling system must also maintain safe engine temperatures during low-speed running, dynamic events, and endurance conditions. Therefore, the cooling system must be treated as both a performance-critical and safety-critical part of the Powertrain system.

    Design Objectives¶

    The cooling system for Bruce was designed to satisfy several engineering objectives at the same time. While the main purpose of the system is to remove heat from the Honda CBR600RR engine, the final design also had to satisfy FSUK rules, fit within the Formula Student chassis, remain manufacturable by the team, and operate reliably during both low-speed and dynamic events. The cooling system was therefore treated as a safety-critical and performance-critical system rather than simply a radiator and a set of hoses.

    The most important requirement is that the engine must remain within a safe operating temperature range during testing and competition. If the cooling system cannot remove enough heat, the engine may suffer from reduced performance, oil breakdown, gasket failure, cylinder head distortion, piston scuffing, or complete mechanical failure. At the same time, the system must use plain water as required by FSUK, contain any overflow safely, and prevent leakage onto the track.

    The main design objectives for the Bruce cooling system are summarised in Table 1. These objectives were used to guide the selection of the radiator, fan, hose routing, catch-can arrangement, pressure cap, mounting method, and manufacturing process.

    Table 1: Main objectives for the Bruce cooling system.

    Overall, the objective was not simply to install the largest possible radiator. The best cooling system for Bruce is the one that provides sufficient heat rejection while remaining compliant, lightweight, manufacturable, serviceable, and reliable. The final design therefore represents a compromise between thermal performance, packaging, airflow, safety, cost, and ease of assembly.

    Design Options and Concept Selection¶

    Before finalising the cooling system design for Bruce several possible design options were considered. The cooling system could not be selected based only on maximum cooling capacity, as the final layout also had to satisfy FSUK rules, fit within the Formula Student chassis, remain lightweight, be manufacturable by the team, and allow easy inspection and maintenance. The purpose of this section is to compare the main design options for the radiator, fan, catch can and overflow system, and hose routing, before explaining why the final cooling system layout was selected.

    The scoring matrices in this section use a simple rating from 1 to 5, where 1 represents a poor match to the requirement and 5 represents a strong match. The scores are not intended to be exact performance values, but they help show the reasoning behind the final concept selection.

    Radiator Options¶

    The radiator is the main heat exchanger in the cooling system, so its selection has a major effect on engine temperature control. Several radiator options were considered, ranging from reusing an original motorcycle radiator to using a larger or custom radiator. The final choice had to provide enough heat rejection for the Honda CBR600RR engine while still fitting within the available space in the car.

    A compact motorcycle radiator was selected as the preferred option. This provided the best overall compromise between cooling capacity, packaging, mass, cost, and availability. A larger radiator would provide more thermal headroom, but it would be more difficult to package, heavier, and could increase drag if the ducting and sidepod design were not optimised. A custom aluminium radiator would be an ideal solution from a design point of view, but the cost, manufacturing time, and uncertainty made it less suitable for the current stage of the project.

    The selected radiator therefore represents a practical Formula Student solution. It is large enough to provide useful cooling capacity, while still being compact enough to fit within the available chassis and bodywork space. This makes it more suitable for Bruce than simply using the largest radiator available

    Fan Options¶

    The cooling fan is required to provide airflow through the radiator when the car is travelling slowly or stationary. This is especially important for Formula Student, where the car may spend time idling, waiting in queues, moving slowly in the paddock, or operating at low speeds during testing. At higher speeds, ram air through the radiator may be sufficient, but at low speeds the fan becomes important for temperature control.

    An OEM-style motorcycle fan was selected as the preferred option. This type of fan is compact, relatively light, easy to source, and suitable for the size of radiator used on Bruce. A motorsport fan would provide strong performance and reliability, but the cost is higher and may not be necessary for the current system. A large automotive fan would provide high airflow, but it would be more difficult to package and would add unnecessary mass.

    The fan should be mounted so that it forces air through the radiator core rather than allowing air to bypass it. The mounting method should avoid damaging the radiator fins or core, and the fan direction should be checked during testing to confirm that it is moving air in the intended direction.

    Table 3: Cooling fan concept comparison matrix

    Catch Can and Overflow Options¶

    The catch can and overflow system are required for FSUK compliance and fluid safety. If the cooling system vents due to pressure rise or local boiling, the expelled water must be collected safely rather than released onto the track. Several catch can options were considered, including a temporary container, a purpose-made catch can, a combined expansion/overflow system, and a lightweight aluminium drinks-can catch can.

    A 1 L aluminium beer-can style catch can was selected as the preferred solution. This option provides a simple, lightweight, low-cost, and easily available container while still meeting the required minimum volume, provided the final can has a capacity of at least 900 ml. Since the cooling system uses water, the container does not need to resist aggressive coolant chemicals, but it still needs to be rigidly mounted, sealed correctly, and routed safely. The aluminium can also makes the system easy to inspect during maintenance, while reducing unnecessary cost compared with purchasing or fabricating a dedicated aluminium catch tank.

    The final catch can must be separate from the engine lubrication catch can. It should be large enough to contain either 10% of the cooling system fluid volume or 900 ml, whichever is greater. For this reason, a 1 L can is suitable, as it provides slightly more than the minimum required volume. The can should be securely mounted using a bracket or clamp so that it cannot move during vehicle operation. It should also be positioned behind the firewall and below the driver’s shoulder level, with the vent routed down to the bottom level of the chassis and exiting outside the bodywork.

    Table 4: Catch can and overflow concept comparison matrix.

    Hose Routing Options¶

    The hose routing determines how coolant travels between the engine, radiator, filler point, and overflow system. Poor hose routing can reduce coolant flow, create air locks, cause leaks, or place hoses near hot or moving components. Several possible hose routing strategies were considered.

    The preferred hose routing option is to use flexible hose with short hard-pipe sections where required. Flexible hose is useful because it can absorb engine movement and vibration, while hard-pipe sections can improve support, reduce long unsupported hose spans, and make the route cleaner. This gives a better balance between reliable coolant flow, packaging, manufacturability, and serviceability.

    The hose route should avoid sharp bends, tight kinks, rubbing points, hot exhaust areas, moving components, and locations where the hose could be damaged during maintenance. Hose clamps should be accessible for inspection and tightening, and the filler or bleed point should be positioned to help remove air from the system. This is important because trapped air can reduce heat transfer and create local hot spots in the cylinder head.

    Table 5: Hose routing concept comparison matrix.

    Final Concept Selection¶

    Based on the comparison of the main design options, the final cooling system concept for Bruce uses a compact motorcycle radiator, an OEM-style cooling fan, a 1 L aluminium beer-can style catch can, and a hose layout based on flexible coolant hose with short supported hard-pipe sections where required. This combination provides the best overall compromise between cooling performance, FSUK compliance, packaging, cost, manufacturability, reliability, and serviceability.

    The selected concept avoids the unnecessary mass and packaging difficulty of a very large radiator, while still providing enough cooling capacity for the CBR600RR engine. The fan improves low-speed cooling performance, which is important for Formula Student operation during idle, testing, and waiting periods before dynamic events. The 1 L aluminium beer-can style catch can provides a lightweight and low-cost method of containing any vented coolant, while still meeting the required minimum overflow volume when mounted and routed correctly. The hose routing strategy allows the system to be assembled and serviced practically, while reducing the risk of kinks, rubbing, and air locks.

    Overall, the chosen cooling system concept is not the most complex or expensive option. Instead, it is the most practical solution for Bruce because it balances thermal performance with rules compliance, manufacturability, reliability, and ease of maintenance. The use of a 1 L aluminium catch can also supports this approach by avoiding unnecessary fabricated parts while still providing a clear, lightweight, and compliant overflow solution.

Since FSUK requires plain water rather than glycol coolant, maintaining pressure is especially important to prevent boiling in hot regions of the cylinder head.

    Final Cooling system design for Bruce¶

    The final cooling system design for Bruce is based around the Honda CBR600RR engine, but the layout has been adapted for the Formula Student chassis rather than copied directly from the original motorcycle. In the CBR600RR motorcycle, the radiator is positioned in direct frontal airflow and is supported by the original fairing and ducting arrangement. In Bruce, the engine is installed in a compact single-seat race car chassis, so the cooling system must be packaged around the sidepod, chassis members, bodywork, driver position, firewall, and FSUK safety requirements.

    Figure 16: Diagram of the original cooling system of the CBR600RR

    Based on the design options and concept selection, the final system uses a compact motorcycle radiator positioned in the sidepod. This was selected as the best compromise between cooling capacity, packaging, mass, cost, and availability. A larger radiator would provide more thermal margin, but it would also be harder to package, heavier, and more dependent on careful ducting. The compact radiator provides a practical solution for Bruce, as it can fit within the available sidepod space while still providing useful heat rejection for the CBR600RR engine.

    A cooling fan is included to support low-speed cooling. This is important because Formula Student cars often operate at relatively low speeds compared with motorcycles. During pit running, staging, repeated testing, acceleration, autocross, and endurance, the car may not always have enough natural airflow through the radiator. The fan therefore provides forced airflow through the radiator core when vehicle speed is low or when the car is stationary. The fan should be mounted so that air is pulled or pushed through the radiator core rather than bypassing it around the edges.

    The coolant circuit uses plain water, as required by Formula Student UK rules. Since glycol-based coolant is not used, the system relies on good radiator performance, reliable airflow, correct pressure control, and careful hose routing to prevent boiling and overheating. The pressure cap is used to pressurise the system, which raises the boiling point of water and improves the boiling margin during operation. The pressure cap and overflow line must therefore be in good condition and routed correctly into the catch can system.

    The final hose routing uses flexible coolant hose with short hard-pipe sections where required. This gives a good balance between flexibility, reliability, and serviceability. Flexible hose allows for engine movement and vibration, while short hard-pipe sections can help support the route, reduce long unsupported hose spans, and improve packaging. The hose route should avoid sharp bends, tight kinks, rubbing points, hot exhaust areas, moving components, and locations where the hose could be damaged during maintenance or operation.

    A dedicated coolant catch can is included as part of the final design. This is required so that any water vented from the cooling system is collected safely rather than released onto the track. The catch can must be separate from the engine lubrication catch can and must be large enough to contain either 10% of the total cooling system volume or 900 ml, whichever is greater. Based on the initial cooling system estimate, the 900 ml FSUK minimum governs the required catch-can volume unless the final measured system volume exceeds 9.0 L.

    The final cooling system layout therefore consists of the CBR600RR water pump, engine coolant passages, sidepod-mounted radiator, cooling fan, flexible hoses, short hard-pipe sections where required, pressure cap, overflow line, and dedicated coolant catch can. Together, these components form a complete thermal management system rather than just a radiator installation. The system must remove heat from the engine, maintain a safe operating temperature, contain hot pressurised water safely, comply with FSUK requirements, and remain accessible for filling, bleeding, inspection, and maintenance.

    Overall, the final design chosen for Bruce is a practical Formula Student cooling system. It avoids the unnecessary mass and packaging difficulty of an oversized radiator while still providing enough cooling potential for the Honda CBR600RR engine. The selected radiator, fan, hose routing, pressure control, and catch-can arrangement provide a balanced solution between thermal performance, rules compliance, manufacturability, reliability, and serviceability.

    Component Availability and Supplier Constraints¶

    Straight coolant hose sections were purchased locally from automotive parts stores. This was a practical choice because straight hose is generally easier to source than specialist pre-formed elbows, and buying it locally reduces delivery time and allows the hose length to be checked and adjusted during assembly. Since the exact hose lengths depend on the final radiator position, engine location, and chassis mounting points, using locally sourced straight sections also makes the system easier to manufacture and modify during fitting.

    The T-connections used in the cooling system can be sourced online from suppliers such as Amazon, depending on the required hose diameter and material.     Hose clamps were sourced from local hardware or automotive stores. These are simple but important components because they secure the coolant hoses to the radiator, engine outlets, T-connections, and any other fittings in the system. Local sourcing is suitable for hose clamps because they are standard parts, available in a wide range of diameters, and easy to replace if damaged or incorrectly sized during assembly.

For this cooling system, eBay allowed the team to find components with suitable dimensions, hose connection positions, and packaging potential for the Formula Student chassis. Since radiator fitment depends heavily on available space, mounting points, and hose routing, using eBay also provided a wider range of possible radiator and fan combinations compared with local suppliers. However, for future teams, it is important to check the condition, dimensions, inlet and outlet sizes, and fan mounting compatibility before purchasing, as second hand parts can vary in quality and may require additional brackets or modifications during installation.

    Overall, the recommended purchasing strategy for the cooling system is to source specialist silicone bends online from suppliers such as Rubberstore.eu, purchase straight hose sections locally from automotive parts stores, and use either online T-connectors or original motorcycle fittings depending on packaging and flow requirements. This approach gives a good compromise between availability, cost, and manufacturability.     Filling, Bleeding, and Leak Checking Procedure¶

    After assembly, the cooling system must be filled, bled, and leak checked before the engine is run properly. This is important because trapped air, loose clamps, or small leaks can cause overheating, pressure loss, or unreliable coolant temperature readings. Since the system on Bruce uses plain water and operates under pressure, this procedure must be carried out carefully before testing.

    Before filling, all hose connections, clamps, radiator fittings, drain points, the pressure cap, and the catch-can overflow line should be inspected. Each hose should be fully pushed onto its connector, with the clamp positioned correctly behind the bead or lip where possible. The hoses should also be checked for kinks, rubbing points, sharp edges, and contact with hot components such as the exhaust manifold. The radiator and fan mounts should be checked to make sure they are secure.

    The system should then be filled slowly with plain water through the highest practical filling point. Filling slowly helps reduce trapped air in the radiator, hoses, cylinder head, and thermostat housing. If bleed screws or high points are present, they should be opened until water flows without large air bubbles. If no bleed screw is available, the hoses can be gently squeezed by hand to help move trapped air towards the filler neck. The water level should be topped up as air leaves the system.

    Once initially filled, the pressure cap should be left off and the engine should be run briefly to begin circulating coolant. During this stage, the coolant level and engine temperature should be monitored carefully. As the water pump circulates coolant and the thermostat begins to open, the coolant level may drop, so the system should be topped up as required. The radiator hoses should begin to warm up, showing that coolant is circulating through the system.

    During warm-up, all hose joints, radiator connections, the water pump area, thermostat housing, filler neck, pressure cap area, and catch-can line should be checked for leaks. Any visible leak, dripping, spray, or smell of hot coolant should be treated as a failure, and the engine should be stopped before further testing.

    Once the coolant level has stabilised and no major air bubbles are visible, the pressure cap can be fitted. The engine can then continue warming up while the system becomes pressurised. This is the most important stage for leak checking because some leaks may only appear when the coolant is hot and under pressure. The fan should also be checked to confirm that air is being moved through the radiator core in the correct direction.

    After the first warm-up cycle, the system should be allowed to cool before the pressure cap is removed. The cap should never be opened while the system is hot, as pressurised water or steam could be released. Once cool, the coolant level should be checked again and topped up if required. All clamps, hoses, radiator mounts, fan mounts, and the catch can should be inspected for movement, leaks, rubbing, or vented coolant.

    Overall, the cooling system should only be considered ready for testing once it has a stable coolant level, no visible leaks, no trapped air pockets, correct fan operation, and stable engine temperature. This procedure should be repeated after any hose replacement, radiator removal, or major cooling system maintenance.

    Validation and Testing Plan¶

    After the cooling system has been assembled, filled, and bled, it must be tested to confirm that it operates reliably on Bruce. The validation process checks that the system does not leak, coolant circulates correctly, the radiator and fan remove heat effectively, and the engine temperature remains within a safe operating range. Since the system contains hot pressurised water, testing should be carried out gradually and safely.

    The first stage is a static visual inspection. Before the engine is started, all hoses, clamps, radiator connections, water pump connections, thermostat housing, filler neck, pressure cap, overflow line, and catch can should be checked. Hoses should be inspected for kinks, rubbing points, sharp edges, and contact with hot components such as the exhaust. The radiator and fan mounts should also be checked for secure attachment.

    The next stage is a static leak test. Once the system has been filled and bled, all cooling joints should be inspected while the car is stationary. Any dripping, wet joints, coolant marks, loose clamps, or water collecting under the car should be treated as a failure. If a leak is found, the system should be safely depressurised, repaired, and tested again.

    The engine should then be started and allowed to warm up gradually while the coolant temperature is monitored. During warm-up, the coolant level, radiator hoses, thermostat operation, and possible leaks should be checked. The radiator hoses should begin to warm up as coolant circulates. If the temperature rises too quickly, becomes unstable, or does not stabilise, the engine should be stopped and the system should be checked for trapped air, poor flow, fan issues, or radiator blockage.

    Fan operation should also be confirmed during this stage. The fan must move air through the radiator core in the correct direction and should not contact the radiator, bodywork, wiring, or hoses. This is especially important because the radiator is positioned in the sidepod, where airflow may be lower than in the original motorcycle installation.

    Once the system passes the warm-up and fan checks, the car should be tested at idle and low engine speed to confirm temperature stability. The coolant temperature should stabilise rather than continuously increase. This is important because Formula Student cars often spend time stationary or moving slowly during pit operation, staging, and testing.

    After static testing, the system should be checked during gentle low-speed driving. The aim is to confirm that the radiator receives airflow and that the hoses and mounts remain secure under vibration and vehicle movement. After the run, the system should be inspected again for leaks, rubbing marks, hose movement, loose clamps, or coolant vented into the catch can.

    The final stage is dynamic testing under more representative Formula Student conditions, including repeated acceleration, braking, cornering, and longer running periods. Coolant temperature should be logged where possible, especially during endurance-style running. The system should maintain stable temperature without excessive venting, pressure loss, leaks, or overheating.

    A post-test inspection should be completed after each major cooling system test. The system should be allowed to cool before the pressure cap is removed. The coolant level, clamps, hoses, radiator mounts, fan mounts, overflow line, and catch can should then be checked. Any coolant loss, rubbing, loose mounts, soft hoses, or repeated temperature rise should be investigated before further running.

    Risks, Issues, and Future Improvements¶

    The cooling system for Bruce is relatively simple compared with some other Powertrain systems, as most of the major components are bought parts and the system does not require complex manufacturing. However, there are still some risks and possible improvements that should be considered during testing and future development.

    The main area for future improvement is the radiator position and angle. The radiator should be positioned so that it receives as much clean airflow as possible through the core. Ideally, the radiator should be mounted as close to horizontal or as directly aligned with the incoming airflow path as the packaging allows. If the radiator is angled poorly, air may pass around the radiator instead of through it, reducing cooling performance. This is especially important because the radiator is mounted in the sidepod rather than in the original motorcycle frontal airflow position.

    Another improvement would be to refine the sidepod ducting around the radiator. Even if the radiator itself is suitable, cooling performance depends strongly on how much air is forced through the core. Any gaps around the radiator may allow air to bypass the fins, reducing heat transfer. Simple sealing panels, ducting, or foam edging could help direct more air through the radiator and improve cooling efficiency without changing the radiator itself.

    The fan and airflow direction should also be checked during testing. The fan must assist airflow through the radiator core rather than pulling or pushing air in the wrong direction. If the fan does not provide enough airflow during low-speed running, pit operation, or staging, the engine temperature may continue to rise when the car is not moving quickly. Future testing should confirm whether the selected fan is sufficient for these conditions.

    The hose routing should also be inspected after the first few heat cycles and test runs. Although the hoses were routed physically on the car, vibration and heat may cause them to settle or move slightly. Any rubbing points, soft hose sections, loose clamps, or areas close to the exhaust should be corrected before further running. This is important because a hose failure could quickly lead to coolant loss and overheating.

    Overall, there are no major design issues expected with the cooling system at this stage. The most important future improvement is to optimise the radiator angle and airflow path so that air is directed through the radiator as efficiently as possible. After this, the system should mainly require normal testing, inspection, and small improvements based on real coolant temperature data from the car.
