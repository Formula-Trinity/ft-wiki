    How an Engine Works¶

    What is an engine?¶

    An engine is a machine that converts energy into mechanical work. In a vehicle, the engine provides the power needed to rotate the drivetrain and move the car. This is done by taking an energy source, such as fuel or electricity, and converting it into rotational motion that can be transmitted through the clutch, gearbox, chain, differential, and finally to the wheels.

    Figure 1: Rotation movement of the engine.

    There are several different types of engines used in vehicles and machinery. The most common type in motorsport and road vehicles is the internal combustion engine, where fuel is burned inside the engine itself to produce power. Other types include electric motors, which convert electrical energy into rotation, external combustion engines such as steam engines, and hybrid systems which combine an internal combustion engine with electric drive components. Although these systems all produce useful mechanical output, they do so using different methods of energy conversion.[1]

    For this document, the focus will be on the internal combustion engine, specifically the Honda CBR600RR engine used in the Formula Student car Bruce. An internal combustion engine works by burning an air-fuel mixture inside the cylinder. This combustion process produces high-pressure and high-temperature gases, which force the piston downwards. The piston is connected to the crankshaft through a connecting rod, so the up-and-down movement of the piston is converted into rotational motion at the crankshaft.

    Figure 2: Image of the Honda CBR600RR engine.

    The Honda CBR600RR engine is a four-stroke petrol engine. This means that each cylinder completes four separate strokes during one full operating cycle: intake, compression, power, and exhaust. During the intake stroke, the piston moves down and draws air and fuel into the cylinder. During the compression stroke, the piston moves back up and compresses this mixture. Near the top of the compression stroke, the spark plug ignites the mixture, causing combustion. The pressure from combustion then forces the piston down during the power stroke, producing useful work. Finally, during the exhaust stroke, the piston moves back up and pushes the burnt gases out through the exhaust valves.[1]

    Figure 3: Engine Diagram showing specific components of the engine.

    The engine is therefore not a single component, but a complete mechanical system made up of many parts working together. The pistons, crankshaft, connecting rods, valves, camshafts, spark plugs, injectors, lubrication system, cooling system, intake system, and exhaust system all have to operate correctly for the engine to produce reliable power. If one of these systems does not work properly, the engine may lose performance, overheat, misfire, suffer mechanical wear, or fail completely.[1]

    In a Formula Student car, the engine must be treated as both a power source and a design constraint. It must produce enough torque and power for acceleration and dynamic events, but it must also be packaged safely inside the chassis, cooled effectively, supplied with fuel and air, lubricated correctly, and controlled by the ECU. Since the CBR600RR engine was originally designed for a motorcycle, its use in Bruce requires careful consideration of mounting, intake layout, exhaust routing, cooling performance, sensor integration, and competition rules. This makes the engine the most important system in the overall powertrain design.

    Parts of the Engine¶

    An engine is made up of many individual components that work together to convert fuel energy into useful mechanical power. Although the engine can be viewed as one complete unit, it is actually a combination of several smaller systems, including the rotating assembly, valvetrain, intake system, exhaust system, cooling system, lubrication system, fuel system, and electrical control system. Each of these sections has a specific function, but they must all operate together for the engine to run reliably and efficiently.[1]

    Engine Block/Crankcase¶

    The engine block, also known as the crankcase in many motorcycle engines, is the main structural body of the engine. It supports and houses many of the most important internal components, including the cylinders, crankshaft, bearings, oil galleries, and coolant passages. Because of this, the engine block must be strong enough to withstand combustion forces, vibration, heat, and the loads produced by the rotating assembly during operation.[1]

    In the Honda CBR600RR engine used in Bruce, the crankcase forms the lower section of the engine and contains the crankshaft and transmission components, while the cylinder section supports the pistons as they move up and down. During combustion, high pressure is produced above the piston, and this force is transferred through the connecting rod into the crankshaft. The engine block must therefore keep all of these moving parts accurately aligned while also providing a sealed structure for oil and coolant flow.

    Figure 4: Image of an engine block for a V8 engine.

    The engine block also plays an important role in heat management. Coolant passages are built into the engine structure so that water can flow around the cylinders and cylinder head, removing heat from the hottest areas of the engine. Oil passages are also built into the crankcase to supply lubrication to the crankshaft bearings, connecting rod bearings, gearbox components, and other moving parts. Without these internal passages, the engine would quickly overheat or suffer from excessive friction and wear.[1]

    Cylinder Head¶

    The cylinder head is the upper section of the engine and sits above the engine block or cylinder section. It forms the top of the combustion chamber and contains many of the components needed for intake, combustion, and exhaust flow. These include the intake ports, exhaust ports, intake valves, exhaust valves, valve springs, camshafts, spark plugs, and parts of the coolant and oil passages. Because of this, the cylinder head is one of the most important parts of the engine for both performance and reliability.[1]

    Figure 5: Diagram showing the cylinder head of an engine.

    During operation, the cylinder head controls how air enters the engine and how exhaust gases leave it. The intake ports guide the fresh air or air-fuel mixture into the cylinder, while the exhaust ports allow the burnt gases to exit after combustion. The valves open and close at precise times, controlled by the camshafts, so that the four-stroke cycle can take place correctly. If the valve timing, sealing, or airflow through the cylinder head is poor, the engine can lose power, run inefficiently, or suffer mechanical damage.[1]

    The cylinder head is also exposed to very high temperatures and pressures. Combustion occurs directly below it, and the exhaust ports carry hot gases away from the cylinder immediately after the power stroke. For this reason, the cylinder head contains coolant passages that allow water to flow around the hottest areas, especially near the combustion chamber, exhaust valves, and valve seats. Oil passages are also required to lubricate the camshafts, valve train, and other moving components in the head.[1]

    In the Honda CBR600RR engine used in Bruce, the cylinder head is especially important because the engine is a high-revving motorcycle engine. It is designed to move air efficiently at high engine speeds, using multiple valves per cylinder and precise camshaft timing. When installed in a Formula Student car, the cylinder head must work correctly with the redesigned intake, exhaust, cooling system, and ECU calibration.

    Cylinders¶

    The cylinders are the internal spaces inside the engine where the pistons move up and down. They form the main working area of the engine, as this is where the air-fuel mixture is compressed, ignited, and converted into useful mechanical power. In a four-stroke internal combustion engine, each cylinder goes through the intake, compression, power, and exhaust strokes, with the piston moving between top dead centre and bottom dead centre during each cycle.[1]

    Figure 6: Cylinders of an engine being re-sleeved

    During operation, the cylinder must guide the piston accurately while also maintaining a strong seal between the piston rings and the cylinder wall. This seal is important because it allows compression pressure to build before ignition and prevents combustion gases from escaping into the crankcase. If the cylinder surface is worn, damaged, or poorly sealed, the engine can lose compression, burn oil, produce less power, or suffer from increased friction and heat.[1]

    The cylinders are exposed to high temperatures and pressures because combustion takes place directly inside them. For this reason, they must be made from materials and surface finishes that can withstand repeated thermal and mechanical loading. Coolant passages around the cylinders help remove heat from the engine structure, while oil lubrication reduces friction between the piston rings and the cylinder wall.[1]

    In the Honda CBR600RR engine used in Bruce, there are four cylinders arranged in an inline-four layout. This means that four pistons operate side by side and deliver power through a shared crankshaft. This arrangement is common in high-revving motorcycle engines because it allows smooth operation, compact packaging, and strong power output at high engine speeds.

    Pistons¶

    The pistons are moving components inside the cylinders that transfer the force produced by combustion to the crankshaft. During operation, the air-fuel mixture is compressed above the piston and then ignited by the spark plug. This combustion creates a rapid increase in pressure, which pushes the piston down the cylinder during the power stroke. The piston is connected to the crankshaft by the connecting rod, allowing this up-and-down motion to be converted into rotational motion.[1]

    Figure 7: Diagram showing parts of the piston head.

    The piston must operate under very demanding conditions. It is exposed to high combustion temperatures, high cylinder pressures, friction against the cylinder wall, and rapid acceleration and deceleration at high engine speeds. To work correctly, the piston must be strong enough to withstand these loads while also being light enough to reduce inertia. This is especially important in a high-revving engine such as the Honda CBR600RR, where the pistons move extremely quickly during operation.[1]

    For Bruce, piston condition is important because any wear or damage can directly affect engine performance and reliability. Damage to the piston crown, skirt, or gudgeon pin area can also create friction, imbalance, or local hot spots, which may lead to further engine damage at high rpm. Therefore, the pistons should be inspected during engine maintenance for signs of scoring, cracks, overheating, carbon build-up, or excessive ring wear, especially after extended dyno running, testing, or competition use.

    Piston Rings¶

    Piston rings are thin circular rings fitted into grooves around the outside of the piston. Their main purpose is to create a seal between the piston and the cylinder wall. This seal is important because it allows the air-fuel mixture to be compressed properly before ignition and helps prevent high-pressure combustion gases from escaping past the piston into the crankcase.[1]

    Figure 8: Image showing the different piston rings

    Piston rings also help control engine oil inside the cylinder. A thin film of oil is needed on the cylinder wall to reduce friction and wear as the piston moves up and down. However, too much oil entering the combustion chamber can cause oil burning, smoke, carbon deposits, and reduced engine performance. The piston rings therefore help scrape excess oil from the cylinder wall while still leaving enough lubrication for reliable operation.[1]

    During engine operation, piston rings are exposed to high temperatures, high pressure, friction, and constant movement. If the rings become worn, damaged, or stuck, the engine may lose compression, burn oil, produce smoke, or suffer from increased crankcase pressure. This can reduce power output and may eventually lead to more serious engine wear.

    In the Honda CBR600RR engine used in Bruce, piston ring condition is important because the engine operates at high rpm and high load during Formula Student events. Good ring sealing helps maintain compression, combustion efficiency, oil control, and overall reliability. Since the engine is being used outside of its original motorcycle application, checking compression and monitoring oil consumption can help indicate whether the piston rings and cylinders are still in good condition.

    Connecting rods¶

    The connecting rods are the components that connect the pistons to the crankshaft. Their main purpose is to transfer the force produced during combustion from the piston to the crankshaft, allowing the engine to convert the piston’s up-and-down movement into rotational motion. Each piston has its own connecting rod, and the lower end of the rod is attached to the crankshaft through a bearing surface.[1]

    Figure 9: Connecting Rod Diagram

    During operation, the connecting rod is exposed to very high forces. When combustion pushes the piston down during the power stroke, the connecting rod is placed under compression as it transfers this load to the crankshaft. During other parts of the engine cycle, especially at high rpm, the rod is also placed under tension as the piston rapidly changes direction at top dead centre and bottom dead centre. Because of this, connecting rods must be strong, stiff, and accurately manufactured.[1]

    The connecting rod also plays an important role in engine smoothness and reliability. If a connecting rod is bent, damaged, or incorrectly fitted, it can cause vibration, uneven piston movement, bearing wear, loss of compression, or serious engine failure. The rod bearings must also receive proper lubrication from the engine oil system, as they operate under high load and rotate at high speed around the crankshaft journal.[1]

    For Bruce, the connecting rods should mainly be considered from a reliability and maintenance point of view. Since the engine is used for repeated high-rpm testing and acceleration events, the rods and their bearings should be checked for early signs of fatigue or lubrication-related damage. Important inspection points include big-end bearing wear, small-end wear, heat discolouration, surface scoring, abnormal play, and any evidence of rod bolt stretching. These checks are important because connecting rod or bearing failure would likely damage the crankshaft, piston, cylinder, and engine cases, making it one of the more serious possible internal engine failures.

    Crankshaft¶

    The crankshaft is one of the main rotating components inside the engine. Its purpose is to convert the up-and-down motion of the pistons into rotational motion that can be used to drive the drivetrain. The pistons are connected to the crankshaft through the connecting rods, so when combustion forces a piston downwards, the connecting rod pushes on the crankshaft and causes it to rotate.

    Figure 10: Parts of a crankshaft

    The crankshaft contains several offset sections called crank throws or crank journals. These allow the straight-line motion of each piston to be converted into rotation. As the crankshaft turns, it also controls the position of each piston inside its cylinder, moving the pistons through the intake, compression, power, and exhaust strokes. In a four-cylinder engine, the crankshaft is designed so that the pistons move in a balanced sequence, helping the engine run smoothly and produce continuous power.[1]

    During operation, the crankshaft is exposed to high torque, bending forces, vibration, and repeated loading from combustion. It must therefore be strong, accurately machined, and properly supported by bearings inside the crankcase. These bearings rely on pressurised engine oil to reduce friction and prevent metal-to-metal contact. If lubrication is poor or the crankshaft bearings are damaged, the engine can suffer from knocking, excessive wear, loss of oil pressure, or complete mechanical failure.[1]

    Camshafts¶

    The camshafts are rotating shafts located in the cylinder head that control the opening and closing of the engine valves. Their main purpose is to ensure that the intake and exhaust valves open at the correct time during the four-stroke cycle. This allows fresh air or air-fuel mixture to enter the cylinder during the intake stroke and allows burnt gases to leave during the exhaust stroke.[1]

    Figure 11: Parts of a Camshaft

    A camshaft contains specially shaped lobes called cams. As the camshaft rotates, these lobes push on the valve mechanism and open the valves against the force of the valve springs. When the cam lobe rotates away, the valve spring closes the valve again. The shape of the cam lobe affects how far the valve opens, how long it stays open, and how quickly it opens and closes. Because of this, camshaft design has a major effect on engine breathing, torque, power output, throttle response, and high-rpm performance.[1]

    The camshafts must remain accurately timed with the crankshaft. This is normally done using a timing chain, belt, or gear system. If the camshaft timing is incorrect, the valves may open too early or too late, causing poor combustion, loss of power, misfiring, or in severe cases contact between the valves and pistons. Correct valve timing is therefore essential for both performance and engine safety.[1]

    For Bruce, the camshafts are mainly important from a performance, timing, and reliability point of view. Since the Honda CBR600RR engine is used in repeated high-rpm conditions, the cam lobes, journals, and valve train components should be inspected for wear, scoring, pitting, or signs of poor lubrication. The cam timing should also be checked whenever the engine is serviced, as incorrect timing could reduce power, affect throttle response, or risk valve-to-piston contact. Valve clearances are also important, as excessive or insufficient clearance can lead to valve noise, poor sealing, reduced compression, or increased wear. Maintaining the camshafts and valve train correctly helps ensure consistent airflow, reliable combustion, and safe engine operation during testing and competition.

    Valves¶

    The valves are components located in the cylinder head that control the flow of gases into and out of the cylinder. In a four-stroke engine, the intake valves allow fresh air or air-fuel mixture to enter the cylinder, while the exhaust valves allow burnt combustion gases to leave after the power stroke. By opening and closing at the correct time, the valves allow the engine to complete the intake, compression, power, and exhaust strokes correctly.[1]

    Figure 12: How Valves are connected to the Camshaft

    The valves are controlled by the camshafts and valve springs. As the camshaft rotates, the cam lobes push the valves open. When the cam lobe moves away, the valve spring forces the valve closed again. When closed, the valve must seal tightly against the valve seat so that compression pressure is maintained inside the cylinder. If a valve does not seal correctly, the engine can lose compression, misfire, run inefficiently, or suffer from reduced power output.[1]

    Valves are exposed to demanding operating conditions. Intake valves must handle repeated airflow and mechanical loading, while exhaust valves are exposed to very hot combustion gases leaving the cylinder. Because of this, valves must be strong, heat-resistant, and accurately timed. Poor valve timing, worn valve seats, weak valve springs, or damaged valve faces can affect engine breathing and may lead to serious mechanical problems.[1]

    For Bruce, valve condition is important for maintaining compression, power delivery, and engine reliability. Since the Honda CBR600RR engine is used in repeated high-rpm and high-load conditions, the valves, valve seats, valve springs, and clearances should be checked during engine maintenance. Poor valve sealing can reduce compression and cause misfires or loss of power, while incorrect valve clearance can lead to valve noise, poor sealing, overheating, or increased wear. Exhaust valves are especially important to monitor because they are exposed to the highest gas temperatures. Regular inspection helps reduce the risk of valve damage, loss of compression, or valve-to-piston contact during testing and competition.

    Spark Plugs¶

    Spark plugs are components fitted into the cylinder head that ignite the compressed air-fuel mixture inside the combustion chamber. In a petrol internal combustion engine, the air and fuel mixture does not ignite by compression alone. Instead, the spark plug produces an electrical spark at the correct moment near the end of the compression stroke, starting the combustion process.[1]

    Figure 13: Image of a Spark Plug

    During operation, the ignition coil supplies a high voltage to the spark plug. This voltage jumps across the small gap between the spark plug electrodes and creates a spark. The spark ignites the compressed air-fuel mixture, causing a rapid increase in temperature and pressure inside the cylinder. This pressure then pushes the piston down during the power stroke, producing useful mechanical work.[1]

    Spark plugs must operate in very harsh conditions. They are exposed to high combustion temperatures, high pressure, fuel, oil vapour, and repeated electrical discharge. If a spark plug is worn, fouled, incorrectly gapped, or damaged, the engine may misfire, lose power, idle poorly, use more fuel, or become harder to start. Correct spark plug condition is therefore important for reliable combustion and efficient engine operation.[1]

    For Bruce, spark plug condition is useful not only for reliability, but also as an indicator of how well the engine is running. Since the Honda CBR600RR engine is used during tuning and repeated high-load events, the plugs should be inspected for electrode wear, incorrect gap, carbon fouling, oil deposits, overheating, or signs of lean/rich running. These checks can help identify issues with fuelling, ignition strength, oil control, or combustion temperature before they lead to misfires or loss of performance. Maintaining correctly gapped and clean spark plugs helps ensure stable combustion, sharp throttle response, and consistent engine operation during testing and competition.

    Fuel Injectors¶

    Fuel injectors are components that deliver fuel into the engine in a controlled and accurate way. Their main purpose is to supply the correct amount of fuel so that it can mix with the incoming air and form a combustible air-fuel mixture. In a petrol engine, this mixture is then compressed inside the cylinder and ignited by the spark plug during the combustion process.[1]

    Figure 14: Fuel Injector Cross Section

    During operation, the ECU controls when each injector opens and how long it stays open for. This opening time is known as the injector pulse width. By changing the pulse width, the ECU can adjust the amount of fuel entering the engine depending on engine speed, throttle position, air flow, temperature, and load. This allows the engine to run correctly during idle, acceleration, steady running, and high-load conditions.[1]

    Fuel injectors must provide a fine and consistent spray pattern so that the fuel can mix properly with the incoming air. If an injector is blocked, leaking, incorrectly sized, or poorly controlled, the engine may run too lean or too rich (Or in the case of bella, with a rotated throttle body, firing into a wall). This can cause poor throttle response, misfires, high fuel consumption, loss of power, overheating, or possible engine damage. Correct injector operation is therefore important for both performance and reliability.[1]

    For Bruce, injector operation is especially important because the Honda CBR600RR engine is being used with a custom intake, exhaust system, and ECU calibration. This means the original fuelling behaviour may no longer match the airflow characteristics of the engine, so injector pulse width, fuel pressure, and air-fuel ratio must be checked carefully during tuning. The injectors should also be inspected for leaks, blockage, poor spray pattern, damaged connectors, or inconsistent flow between cylinders. Poor injector performance can cause one cylinder to run lean or rich compared with the others, leading to misfires, overheating, reduced power, or possible engine damage. Ensuring consistent injector operation helps improve throttle response, combustion stability, and reliability during dyno testing, acceleration runs, and competition use

    Petrol Engine¶

    A petrol engine is a type of internal combustion engine that uses petrol as its fuel and a spark plug to ignite the air-fuel mixture. In this type of engine, air and fuel are mixed together before or during the intake process, compressed inside the cylinder, and then ignited by an electrical spark. The combustion process produces a rapid increase in pressure and temperature, forcing the piston downwards and producing mechanical work at the crankshaft. [2]

    Figure 15: Four stroke cycle of a petrol engine.

    Most petrol engines operate using the four-stroke cycle, which consists of the intake, compression, power, and exhaust strokes. During the intake stroke, the piston moves down and the intake valves open, allowing air or an air-fuel mixture to enter the cylinder. During the compression stroke, the piston moves back up and compresses the mixture. Near the top of the compression stroke, the spark plug ignites the mixture, causing combustion. During the power stroke, the expanding gases push the piston down and rotate the crankshaft. Finally, during the exhaust stroke, the exhaust valves open and the piston pushes the burnt gases out of the cylinder.[2]

    Figure 16: Petrol Engine used in a FSUK car.

    Petrol engines are commonly used in motorcycles, cars, and motorsport applications because they can be compact, relatively lightweight, and capable of producing high power at high engine speeds. Compared with diesel engines, petrol engines usually operate at lower compression ratios and rely on spark ignition rather than compression ignition. This allows them to rev more freely and respond quickly to throttle input, which is useful in performance applications.[2]

    The Honda CBR600RR engine used in Bruce is a petrol engine. It is a high-revving four cylinder motorcycle engine, meaning it is designed to produce power efficiently at high rpm while remaining compact and lightweight. When used in a Formula Student car, the petrol engine must be supported by several other systems, including the intake, exhaust, fuel injection, ignition, cooling, lubrication, and ECU control systems. These systems all work together to allow the engine to produce reliable power during acceleration, autocross, endurance, and testing conditions.

    Diesel Engine¶

    A diesel engine is a type of internal combustion engine that uses diesel fuel and compression ignition to produce power. Unlike a petrol engine, a diesel engine does not normally use a spark plug to ignite the air-fuel mixture. Instead, only air is drawn into the cylinder and compressed to a very high pressure. This compression raises the temperature of the air significantly. Diesel fuel is then injected directly into the hot compressed air, where it ignites automatically.[2]

    Figure 17: Four stroke cycle of a Diesel Engine.

    Most diesel engines operate using the same four-stroke cycle as petrol engines: intake, compression, power, and exhaust. During the intake stroke, the piston moves down and draws air into the cylinder. During the compression stroke, the piston moves up and compresses this air to a much higher pressure than in a petrol engine. Near the end of the compression stroke, diesel fuel is injected into the cylinder. The heat from the compressed air causes the fuel to ignite, producing high pressure that forces the piston down during the power stroke. Finally, during the exhaust stroke, the burnt gases are pushed out through the exhaust valves.[2]

    Figure 18: Massive Diesel Engine used in a ship.

    Diesel engines are commonly used in trucks, buses, ships, generators, and heavy machinery because they are strong, efficient, and able to produce high torque at low engine speeds. Compared with petrol engines, diesel engines usually have higher compression ratios, heavier components, and stronger engine blocks because they must withstand greater cylinder pressures. They are often more fuel efficient, but they are usually heavier and do not rev as high as petrol engines.[2]

    Although diesel engines are very useful in many engineering applications, they are less common in lightweight motorsport applications such as Formula Student combustion cars. This is because diesel engines are usually heavier, slower-revving, and harder to package compared with small petrol motorcycle engines.[2]

    Most Common FSUK Engines¶

    In Formula Student UK, combustion cars have traditionally used small motorcycle engines because they are compact, lightweight, high-revving, and already include many of the components needed for a race vehicle, such as the clutch, gearbox, starter system, charging system, and engine sensors. Most teams choose engines below the maximum displacement limit and then design their intake, exhaust, cooling, fuel, and ECU systems around the requirements of the Formula Student rules. Because the engine is restricted by the intake system, the aim is not only to choose the engine with the highest original motorcycle power output, but to choose an engine that is reliable, easy to package, easy to tune, and suitable for the car’s expected operating range.[3]

    Figure 19: Image of a Suzuki GSX-R600

    The most common type of engine used in combustion Formula Student cars is a 600 cc inline four motorcycle engine. Examples include the Honda CBR600RR, Yamaha YZF-R6, Suzuki GSX-R600, and Kawasaki ZX-6R. These engines are popular because they offer a good balance of power, mass, availability, and aftermarket support. They are designed to operate at high rpm and can produce strong power from a small package, which makes them suitable for lightweight Formula Student cars. However, because Formula Student cars operate with an intake restrictor and spend a lot of time accelerating from low and medium speeds, these engines must often be tuned for drivability and mid-range torque rather than only peak power.[3]

    Figure 20: Image of a Honda CBR600RR

    The Honda CBR600RR is one of the most recognisable engine choices in Formula Student and is the engine used in Bruce. It is a 599 cc inline-four petrol motorcycle engine, making it compact, lightweight, and capable of high engine speeds. Other teams have also used similar 600 cc engines such as the Suzuki GSX-R600 and Yamaha R6. These engines all follow the same general idea: a high-revving four-cylinder motorcycle engine adapted from its original motorcycle application into a single-seat race car.[3]

    Figure 21: Image of a KTM 690

    Some Formula Student teams also use single-cylinder engines, with the KTM 690 being one of the most common examples. A single-cylinder engine is very different from a 600 cc inline-four. It usually has fewer moving parts, lower mass, simpler packaging, and stronger low-end torque, but it does not usually rev as high or produce the same smooth high-rpm power delivery as a four-cylinder engine. For Formula Student, this can still be an advantage because the car needs strong throttle response, low weight, and good acceleration out of corners rather than only maximum top-end power.[3]

    Figure 22: Image of a Kawasaki ZX-6R

    Overall, the most common combustion engine choices in Formula Student are high-revving 600 cc motorcycle engines, especially inline-four engines from Honda, Yamaha, Suzuki, and Kawasaki. Single-cylinder engines such as the KTM 690 are also used by some teams as a lighter and simpler alternative. For Bruce, the Honda CBR600RR is a suitable choice because it provides a strong combination of compact packaging, high-rpm capability, good parts availability, and proven use in Formula Student-style applications.[3]
