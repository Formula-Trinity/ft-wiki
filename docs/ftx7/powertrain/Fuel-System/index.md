# Fuel System

## What is a fuel supply system and why do we need it?

The fuel supply system is the part of an internal combustion engine setup responsible for storing
fuel and delivering it to the engine in a controlled and reliable way. In a petrol engine, fuel must
be supplied from the tank to the injectors at the correct pressure so that the engine can produce
the required air-fuel mixture for combustion. Without a working fuel supply system, the engine
would not receive fuel and therefore would not be able to run.

Figure 1: More in depth diagram of a fuel system.

In a Formula Student car, the fuel supply system has to do more than simply hold petrol. It must
store enough fuel for testing and competition events, deliver fuel consistently during acceleration,
braking, and cornering, prevent fuel starvation, and remain safe in the event of vibration, heat,
or impact. The system must also be packaged within a very limited space inside the chassis while
still allowing access for filling, inspection, draining, and maintenance.

Figure 2: Carbon Fiber Fuel tank used in FSAE

Overall, the fuel supply system is a critical part of Formula Student powertrain. It supports
reliable combustion, prevents fuel starvation, improves safety, and allows the Honda CBR600RR
engine to operate correctly in a Formula Student environment. A good fuel system should
therefore be compact, lightweight, leak-free, easy to inspect, and capable of supplying fuel
consistently under all expected driving conditions.

## Main components of a fuel supply system

The fuel supply system is made up of several components that work together to store, control,
and deliver fuel to the engine. Although the fuel tank is the largest and most visible part of the
system, it cannot operate by itself. The tank, filler neck, fuel pump, pressure regulator, fuel lines,
filter, fuel rail, injectors, breather system, drain plug, and anti-slosh system all have to work
together to provide a safe and consistent supply of petrol.

For Bruce, which uses a Honda CBR600RR engine, these components must be adapted from
a motorcycle-style layout into a Formula Student chassis. This means that the system must
provide the correct fuel pressure and flow rate while also being compact, lightweight, safe, easy
to inspect, and reliable during acceleration, braking, cornering, and endurance running.

### Fuel tank

The fuel tank is the main storage container for the petrol used by the engine. Its purpose is to
hold enough fuel for testing and competition while keeping the fuel safely contained inside the
car. The tank must be sealed correctly, securely mounted, resistant to vibration, and positioned
safely within the chassis.

<img width="572" height="272" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/5789141c-154a-4450-9f14-afab993fae74" />

Figure 3: Sample FSAE fuel tank

For a FS car, the tank does not need to be as large as a normal road vehicle or motorcycle fuel
tank. It only needs to contain enough fuel for Formula Student events, with a suitable safety
margin. A smaller tank reduces unnecessary fuel mass and improves packaging, but it must still
provide enough capacity to avoid running out of fuel during endurance.

### Fuel filler neck and cap

The fuel filler neck is the section used to fill the tank. It connects the filler opening to the main
tank body and must be positioned so that the car can be fuelled safely and easily. The filler cap
must seal securely so that fuel cannot escape during driving, vibration, or cornering.

<img width="527" height="307" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/57b814e6-1de5-4f04-a506-0c80e82e469f" />

Figure 4: Sample fuel filler neck and cap

For a FS car, the filler neck is also important for inspection. It must be accessible and designed
so that the fuel level can be checked safely.

### Sight Tube

The sight tube is used to show the fuel level inside the tank. It is usually a clear petrol-compatible
tube connected to the tank so that the fuel level can be seen externally. This makes it easier to
check the amount of fuel in the car without opening the tank.

<img width="472" height="325" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/d0524fe9-ef48-4fb1-a6b0-c674c9483994" />

Figure 5: Sample sight tube in a FSAE fuel tank.

For a FS car, the sight tube must be positioned so that it gives a useful indication of the working
fuel range. If the tank is made smaller than the previous design, the sight tube should still allow
the team and scrutineers to clearly see the relevant fuel level during filling and inspection.

### Fuel Pump

The fuel pump moves petrol from the tank to the engine. In a fuel-injected petrol engine, the
pump is especially important because the injectors require fuel at a controlled pressure and flow
rate. Without a suitable pump, the engine may not receive enough fuel, especially at high rpm
or high load.

<img width="612" height="500" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/ad25c98f-372c-4165-b9b3-eeb38fa3b481" />

Figure 6: Diagram of a fuel pump

The pump can be mounted inside the tank or externally, depending on the final design. For a FS
car, the pump must be suitable for the Honda CBR600RR engine and must maintain stable fuel
delivery during acceleration, braking, and cornering. It must also be mounted securely so that
vibration or impact does not damage the pump or fuel lines.

### Fuel Pressure Regulator

The fuel pressure regulator controls the pressure of the fuel supplied to the injectors. This is
important because the ECU controls fuelling by opening the injectors for a specific amount of
time, but the actual amount of fuel delivered also depends on the fuel pressure.

<img width="670" height="395" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/1aab59bb-b94c-4ecb-a43d-cc66178c7fde" />

Figure 7: Sample fuel pressure regulator

For a FS car, the regulator must keep the fuel pressure stable so that the engine can be tuned
reliably. A fixed-pressure regulator is simple and cost-effective, while an adjustable regulator
allows the pressure to be fine-tuned during testing. The final choice depends on the selected
pump, packaging space, cost, and whether fuel pressure adjustment is required.

### Fuel Injectors

Fuel injectors are electrically controlled valves that deliver fuel into the engine. The ECU opens
and closes the injectors depending on engine speed, throttle position, load, temperature, and
other sensor inputs. The time that the injector stays open is known as the injector pulse width.

<img width="260" height="292" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/1ba08fc8-45f2-4316-b26f-1a11f0ffe7e4" />

Figure 8: Image of a Fuel Injector

For a FS car, the injectors must receive stable fuel pressure so that each cylinder gets the correct
amount of fuel. If the pressure is unstable, the engine may run too lean or too rich, which can
reduce performance, cause misfires, or damage the engine.

### Fuel Lines and Fittings

Fuel lines carry petrol between the tank, pump, regulator, filter, fuel rail, and injectors. They
must be rated for petrol, suitable for the system pressure, and connected using appropriate
fittings or clamps.

<img width="432" height="377" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/60ccbda5-6b2b-4e21-8689-155105c42d6b" />

Figure 9: Image of fuel lines

For a FS car, fuel line routing is very important because the lines must avoid hot components,
sharp edges, moving parts, and areas where they could rub against the chassis. Poor hose routing
or unsuitable fittings could cause leaks, pressure loss, or hose detachment, which would be a
major safety risk.

### Fuel Rail

The fuel rail distributes pressurised fuel to the injectors. It acts as a common supply chamber so
that each injector receives fuel at approximately the same pressure. This is especially important
in a multi-cylinder engine, where each cylinder must receive a consistent fuel supply.

<img width="410" height="132" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/e3ccde9b-8843-47a5-b95d-7d171396b7c2" />

Figure 10: Image of fuel rail

For a FS car, the fuel rail must be compatible with the Honda CBR600RR injectors and the
modified Formula Student intake arrangement. If the original fuel rail is reused, it should be
checked for correct mounting, sealing, and connection to the new fuel lines.

### Fuel filter

The fuel filter removes dirt, debris, and small particles from the fuel before it reaches the injectors.
This is important because injectors have very small openings which can become blocked by
contamination.

<img width="380" height="465" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/e5e8114e-e949-4ac3-8dd1-cc2524cb1f07" />

Figure 11: Image of a fuel filter

A fuel filter is especially important when using a custom-manufactured tank. Cutting, drilling,
welding, and fitting installation can leave small particles inside the tank, even after cleaning.
The filter should therefore be installed in an accessible location and must be rated for the fuel
pressure and flow rate of the system.

### Tank Breather and Vent System

The fuel tank requires a breather or vent system so that air can enter and leave the tank as the
fuel level changes. As fuel is used by the engine, air must enter the tank to prevent a vacuum
forming. If the tank cannot breathe correctly, fuel flow to the pump may be reduced.

<img width="452" height="295" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/ff180906-afe2-446e-84d5-df6f79c93d3c" />

Figure 12: Image of a sample fuel tank breather

The breather system must also prevent fuel from spilling during acceleration, braking, cornering,
or vibration. For Bruce, the vent should be routed safely away from hot components and designed
so that pressure can equalise without allowing liquid fuel to escape.

### Drain Plug

The drain plug allows the fuel tank to be emptied safely for maintenance, inspection, storage, or
fuel changes. Without a proper drain point, removing fuel from the tank can become difficult
and unsafe.

<img width="441" height="372" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/2c9c5feb-76c9-47ed-9bbb-118a79aee2bc" />

Figure 13: Fuel tank being drained via a drain plug

For a FS car, the drain plug should ideally be located at the lowest practical point of the tank so
that as much fuel as possible can be removed. It must seal properly using a suitable fuel-resistant
sealing method, such as a copper washer or threaded fitting, and it should be checked carefully
during leak testing.

### Anti-slosh system

Fuel slosh is the movement of fuel inside the tank during acceleration, braking, cornering, and
vibration. If the fuel moves away from the pump pickup, the pump may draw in air instead of
fuel, causing a temporary loss of pressure. This is known as fuel starvation.

<img width="731" height="405" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/6fafa521-0600-40b5-b47e-907cb89a5df5" />

Figure 14: Three most common methods used for Anti-slosh systems

Anti-slosh systems are used to reduce this risk. Internal baffles can be used, but they add
manufacturing complexity because they must be cut, positioned, and welded inside the tank. For
a FS car, fuel tank foam is a more practical solution because it is simple to install, relatively
cheap, and helps reduce fuel movement without making the tank much harder to manufacture.

## FSUK Fuel System Rules and Safety Requirements

The fuel supply system in a Formula Student car is safety-critical because it stores and transports
flammable liquid close to the engine, exhaust, electrical components, and driver. For this reason,
the fuel tank and all related components must be designed with the FSUK rules in mind from
the start. The rules help reduce the risk of fuel leaks, fire, unsafe refuelling, poor inspection
access, and failure during dynamic events.

For Bruce, the fuel system must be designed around the Honda CBR600RR engine while also
fitting safely within the Formula Student chassis. Since the engine was originally designed for a
motorcycle, the original fuel tank arrangement cannot simply be copied into the car. The tank,
filler neck, pump, pressure regulator, fuel lines, breather, drain plug, and anti-slosh system must
all be installed in a way that is safe, inspectable, and suitable for competition use.

One of the main requirements is the location of the fuel tank. The tank must be mounted within
the protected volume of the chassis, commonly referred to as the roll bar bubble. This helps
protect the tank from impact in the event of a collision, spin, or contact with another object.
The tank must also be mounted securely using proper brackets, fasteners, and support points so
that it cannot move, vibrate excessively, or damage the fuel lines during driving.

Clearance from hot components is also essential. The fuel tank, lines, pump, regulator, and fittings
must be kept away from the exhaust system, as the exhaust can reach very high temperatures
during operation. For Bruce, the fuel system should be kept at least 50 mm away from the
exhaust wherever possible, with heat shielding used where packaging space is limited. This
reduces the risk of fuel heating, vapour lock, and fire if a leak occurs.

The filler neck and cap must allow the tank to be filled safely while preventing fuel from escaping
during driving. The filler should be positioned so that fuel cannot spill onto the engine, exhaust,
wiring, or driver. The cap must seal securely, and the filler neck must work with the sight tube
so that the fuel level can be inspected clearly during scrutineering. For FSUK cars, the filler
neck, sight tube, tank height, and fuel level line should therefore be designed together rather
than treated as separate parts.

The fuel pump and fuel lines must also be installed securely. The pump must not be able to
move during acceleration, braking, cornering, or vibration, as this could damage wiring, fuel lines,
or the tank itself. The fuel lines must be fuel-rated, pressure-rated, and routed away from hot,
sharp, or moving components such as the exhaust, chain, sprocket, suspension, and chassis edges.
They should be supported with clips or brackets where required to prevent rubbing and fatigue.

Leak prevention is one of the most important safety requirements. All welded joints, threaded
fittings, hose connections, injector seals, regulator connections, drain plugs, and pump seals must
be checked before the car is run. The tank should be leak tested before installation, and the full
system should be checked again once installed in the car. Even a small petrol leak can create
fuel vapour, which could ignite if it reaches a hot surface or electrical spark.

The fuel tank must also include a safe method of draining and venting. A drain plug should ideally
be placed at the lowest practical point of the tank so that fuel can be removed for maintenance,
storage, inspection, or transport. It must be sealed correctly, for example using a threaded boss
and copper washer, and included in the leak test. The breather system must allow air to enter
the tank as fuel is used, while preventing fuel from spilling out during braking, cornering, or
when the tank is full.

Fuel starvation must also be considered. Even if the tank contains enough fuel overall, the pump
can still draw in air if fuel moves away from the pickup during acceleration, braking, or cornering.
For Bruce, fuel tank foam is a practical anti-slosh solution because it reduces fuel movement
without requiring complicated internal baffles or extra welding. This helps maintain more stable
fuel delivery to the pump during dynamic events.

The fuel capacity must be large enough to complete endurance without refuelling, but it should
not be unnecessarily oversized. Extra fuel adds mass and reduces vehicle performance, so the
tank should be sized using the expected endurance distance, fuel consumption, and a suitable
safety margin. For Bruce, this means reducing the previous 15 litre tank capacity while still
carrying enough fuel to complete the event reliably.

Before final use, the fuel system should be tested in stages. The tank should first be checked
outside the car for leaks at the welds, filler neck, sight tube, drain plug, fittings, and pump mount.
Once installed, the lines, regulator, rail, and injectors should be inspected, and the pump should
be tested to confirm correct fuel delivery and pressure. Only after these checks should the engine
be run.

Overall, the FSUK fuel system requirements affect almost every part of the design. The system
must be compact and lightweight, but it must also be safe, leak-free, properly mounted, protected
from heat, easy to inspect, and reliable during all Formula Student events.

## Design Objectives

The fuel supply system for Bruce was designed to satisfy several engineering objectives at the
same time. While the main purpose of the system is to store petrol and deliver it to the Honda
CBR600RR engine at the correct pressure and flow rate, the final design also had to satisfy
FSUK safety requirements, fit within the Formula Student chassis, remain manufacturable by the
team, and allow safe filling, draining, inspection, and maintenance. The fuel system was therefore
treated as a safety-critical and performance-critical part of the Powertrain system rather than
simply a fuel tank and a set of hoses.

The most important requirement is that the engine must receive a stable fuel supply during
idle, acceleration, braking, cornering, and endurance running. If the system cannot maintain
fuel pressure, the engine may run lean, misfire, lose power, or suffer damage under high-load
conditions. At the same time, because petrol is flammable, the system must be designed to
minimise the risk of leaks, fuel contact with hot components, poor venting, and unsafe refuelling.

Based on the design matrices, the selected fuel system concept for Bruce uses a compact 5 L steel
fuel tank, a suitable CBR600RR-compatible fuel pump, a fixed 50 psi fuel pressure regulator,
and petrol-compatible fuel tank foam. This combination gives a practical balance between safety,
fuel delivery reliability, low mass, manufacturability, serviceability, and cost.

The final concept avoids the unnecessary mass of the previous larger fuel tank while still providing

fabrication risk, the selected pump and regulator maintain stable fuel delivery, and fuel foam
helps reduce the chance of starvation during acceleration, braking, and cornering. The final
design should still be validated through CAD volume checks, leak testing, pump pressure testing,
and monitored engine running before competition use.

### Calculation Summary

The fuel system calculations show that Bruce does not require a large fuel tank to complete
the endurance event. Based on a conservative fuel economy estimate of 10 km/L over a 22 km
distance, the theoretical fuel requirement is approximately 2.2 L. After allowing for safety margin,
unusable fuel, mapping variation, and dynamic fuel movement, a 5 L tank is a more suitable

model should be updated and checked using the SolidWorks volume or mass properties tool
to confirm that the internal capacity is close to the 5 L target before the aluminium tank is
manufactured.

## Final Fuel System Design for Bruce

The fuel supply system for Bruce was designed around the requirements of a Formula Student
car using a Honda CBR600RR engine. Since this engine was originally designed for a motorcycle,
the original fuel system layout cannot be directly transferred into the car without modification.
In the motorcycle, the fuel tank, pump, filler, breather, and fuel lines are packaged around the
bike frame and rider position. In Bruce, these components must instead be packaged within
the Formula Student chassis while still providing reliable fuel delivery, safe fuel storage, and
compliance with the relevant competition requirements.

The main purpose of the fuel supply system is to provide the engine with a constant supply of
petrol at the correct pressure and flow rate. The Honda CBR600RR is a high-revving four-cylinder
petrol engine, so stable fuelling is required during idle, acceleration, high-load running, and rapid
throttle changes. If the system cannot maintain fuel pressure, or if the pump draws in air, the
engine may run lean, misfire, lose power, or suffer damage. For this reason, the tank, pump,
regulator, fuel lines, injectors, and anti-slosh system must be considered as one complete system
rather than as separate individual components.

The fuel tank for Bruce must be compact, lightweight, safe, and large enough to complete the
Formula Student endurance event. The endurance event is approximately 22 km long, and
refuelling is not allowed during the event. Although the Honda CBR600RR fuel consumption
under normal road conditions can be used as a rough starting point, Bruce will operate under
very different conditions, including repeated acceleration, braking, cornering, low-speed running,
and high-load operation. These conditions are much more demanding than steady road riding,
so a conservative estimate must be used. Based on the initial calculations, around 3 litres of
fuel would likely be enough to complete the endurance distance, but this would leave very little
margin for tuning changes, unusable fuel in the tank, inefficient running, or unexpected fuel
consumption. For this reason, a 5 litre tank was selected as a safer target.

This is a major improvement compared with the previous fuel tank design, which was approxi
mately 15 litres. For a Formula Student car, this is much more fuel capacity than is realistically
required. Carrying unnecessary fuel increases vehicle mass and negatively affects acceleration,
braking, and handling. Since petrol has a density of roughly 0.74 kg/L, reducing the tank capacity
by 10 litres could reduce the maximum fuel mass by approximately 7.4 kg, not including any
additional weight saving from making the tank physically smaller. The 5 litre target therefore
provides a better compromise between reducing unnecessary mass and keeping enough reserve
capacity to complete endurance reliably.

The tank must also be packaged safely within Bruce’s chassis. It should be securely mounted so
that it cannot move during vibration, driving, or impact. The fuel lines must be routed away
from hot components, especially the exhaust, and protected from sharp edges or possible damage.
The filler neck must be accessible from outside the car so that the tank can be filled safely, while
the drain plug should be located at the lowest practical point to allow fuel to be removed when
required. The pump pickup should be positioned where fuel is most likely to collect during
operation, because a poor pickup location could allow the pump to draw in air during cornering,
braking, or when the fuel level is low.

For the tank material, steel was considered the most practical option for Bruce. Aluminium
is lighter, but for a small 5 litre tank the total weight saving may not be large enough to
justify the extra manufacturing difficulty. Steel is easier to weld, easier to obtain, cheaper, and
more forgiving during fabrication. Since the fuel tank is a safety-critical component, producing
strong and leak-free welds is more important than achieving the absolute lowest possible mass.
Aluminium could still be used if the team has the correct welding capability, but steel reduces
manufacturing risk and is therefore a sensible choice for the current design.

The fuel pump must be capable of providing enough pressure and flow for the Honda CBR600RR
engine. The stock Honda pump has the advantage of being originally designed for the engine,
meaning the pressure and flow requirements are already well matched. However, it may be
difficult to package depending on the final tank shape. An aftermarket motorsport pump could
provide more flexibility, especially if a compact external or internal pump is easier to mount.
The pump should not be oversized unnecessarily, as this can increase cost, heat, current draw,
and complexity, but it must still maintain stable fuel pressure at high rpm and high load.

A fuel pressure regulator is also required to keep the injector pressure stable. A target pressure
of approximately 50 psi was considered for the system. A fixed 50 psi regulator would be simple,
reliable, and cost-effective if the required pressure is already known. An adjustable regulator
would allow fine-tuning during testing and mapping, but it would also be more expensive and
may not be necessary unless the fuel system differs significantly from the original motorcycle
setup. For Bruce, a fixed regulator is likely to be the better option unless later testing shows
that fuel pressure adjustment is required.

Fuel slosh is another important design consideration. During acceleration, braking, and cornering,
fuel can move around inside the tank and uncover the pump pickup, even if there is enough
fuel in the tank overall. This can cause pressure fluctuations, fuel starvation, and unstable
engine running. Internal baffles could be used to reduce fuel movement, but they would increase
manufacturing complexity and make the small tank harder to fabricate and inspect. For Bruce,
fuel tank foam is a more practical solution. The foam reduces the free movement of fuel inside
the tank, helps keep fuel near the pickup, and is easier to install than welded baffles.

Overall, the fuel supply system for Bruce prioritises reliability, safety, low mass, and ease of
manufacture. The 5 litre tank provides enough capacity for the endurance event with a safety
margin, while avoiding the unnecessary weight of the previous 15 litre design. Steel construction
reduces manufacturing risk, the pump and regulator ensure stable fuel delivery, and fuel foam
helps control slosh during dynamic driving. The final system should therefore be compact, safe,
inspectable, and capable of supplying the Honda CBR600RR engine under the expected Formula
Student operating conditions.

## Reccommended retailers/component shopping

After the fuel tank size and general layout were considered, the next step was to identify realistic
components that could be purchased and installed in Bruce. This is an important part of the
design process because the final fuel system must be based on parts that are available, affordable,
fuel-compatible, and suitable for the Honda CBR600RR engine. The design cannot be based
only on ideal CAD geometry, as the final system must also consider pump size, pressure rating,
hose compatibility, delivery time, cost, and ease of installation.

The main components that need to be sourced are the fuel pump, fuel pressure regulator, fuel
tank foam, fuel hose, fittings, fuel filter, drain plug, filler cap, sight tube, and sealing hardware.
Since petrol is flammable and the fuel system is located close to the engine and exhaust, all parts
must be rated for fuel use and suitable for the pressure and temperature conditions of the car.

For the fuel pump, one possible option is to use a compact aftermarket pump from DeatschWerks.
A small motorsport-style pump is useful because it is easier to package inside or near a small
Formula Student fuel tank. However, the selected pump must still be checked to make sure it
can provide enough fuel flow and pressure for the Honda CBR600RR engine at high rpm and
high load. Another possible option is to reuse the original Honda CBR600RR fuel pump, as it
was designed for the engine and may reduce the risk of incorrect pressure or flow selection. The
main disadvantage is that it may be harder to package inside a custom fuel tank.

The fuel pressure regulator should be selected to maintain stable fuel pressure at the injectors.
A target pressure of around 50 psi was considered for the system. A fixed 50 psi regulator is a
simple and cost-effective option, with possible suppliers including Fuel Pump Factory and eBay.
An adjustable regulator from a supplier such as DeatschWerks could also be used if fuel pressure
tuning is required during testing, but this may be unnecessarily expensive for the first version of
the system.

Fuel tank foam was selected as the preferred anti-slosh solution. Compared with internal baffles,
foam is easier to install and avoids extra welding inside the tank. Since Bruce uses a relatively
small tank, this is a practical way to reduce fuel movement during acceleration, braking, and
cornering. GT2i was identified as a suitable supplier for motorsport fuel tank foam compatible
with petrol applications.

Fuel hose, fittings, and filters should be sourced from motorsport or automotive suppliers rather
than using general-purpose tubing. The hose must be petrol-resistant and rated for the system
pressure, while the fittings must match the selected pump, regulator, fuel rail, and filter. A fuel
filter should also be included to protect the injectors from debris, especially because the tank is
custom manufactured and may contain small particles from cutting, welding, or drilling.

The drain plug, filler cap, sight tube, and sealing hardware should also be selected carefully. A
threaded drain plug with a copper washer is a practical option because it allows the tank to be
drained for maintenance or inspection while still providing a reliable seal. The filler cap must
seal securely, and the sight tube must be made from petrol-compatible material so that the fuel
level can be checked safely.

Overall, the recommended retailers for the Bruce fuel system include DeatschWerks for pump
and regulator options, Fuel Pump Factory and eBay for fixed 50 psi regulators, GT2i for fuel
tank foam, and general motorsport suppliers for fuel hose, fittings, filters, and sealing hardware.
The final component choice should be confirmed based on pressure rating, fuel compatibility,
packaging space, delivery time, cost, and suitability for the Honda CBR600RR engine.

## Manufacturing and Assembly Process

The fuel tank for Bruce was manufactured from aluminium sheet and assembled as a custom
tank to suit the available chassis space, fuel capacity target, filler neck position, drain position,
and fuel line routing. The manufacturing process was kept as simple as possible so that the
tank could be produced using the tools and workshop resources available to the team. Since the
fuel tank is a safety-critical component, the main priorities during manufacture were accurate
marking, clean cuts, good fit-up between panels, suitable welding, and careful inspection before
the tank was installed on the car.

Before any cutting was carried out, the tank panels were marked out from the CAD dimensions.
The aluminium sheet was measured carefully and the cut lines were marked using a ruler, marker,
and scribe. Each panel had to be checked before cutting because small errors in the panel shape
could make the tank harder to weld and could also affect the final volume of the tank. The
filler neck position, drain plug position, breather position, fuel outlet, and any sight tube fittings
were also considered at this stage so that the tank would still be easy to fill, drain, inspect, and
connect to the rest of the fuel system once fitted to the car.

The main tank panels were cut using an angle grinder. A suitable cutting disc was used and
the aluminium sheet was clamped securely before cutting so that the material could not move
during the operation. The cuts were made gradually along the marked lines to avoid removing
too much material or overheating the sheet. After cutting, the edges of each panel were cleaned
and deburred. This was important because sharp edges could affect weld quality, make the tank
harder to assemble, and create a safety hazard during handling.

Once the panels had been cut, the tank was trial-fitted before welding. The panels were placed
together to check that the edges aligned correctly and that the overall shape matched the intended
design. Any small adjustments were made before welding so that large gaps were avoided. Good
fit-up was important because the fuel tank needed to be sealed fully around all welded joints.
Poor panel alignment would make welding more difficult and could increase the risk of pinholes
or leaks.

The aluminium panels were then welded together to form the tank body. The welds were made
around the outside edges of the tank, joining each panel into a sealed structure. During welding,
care had to be taken to avoid distortion of the aluminium sheet, as excessive heat could cause
the panels to warp and make the tank harder to fit into the chassis. The welded joints were
inspected visually after welding for gaps, missed sections, cracks, or areas that looked likely to
leak. Since the tank stores petrol, any poor weld quality would need to be corrected before the
tank could be used.

After the main tank body had been welded, the required holes were drilled for the fuel system
fittings. These holes included the filler neck, drain plug, breather or vent connection, fuel outlet
or pump connection, and sight tube connections where required. The holes were drilled carefully
to the required size and were deburred after drilling. Removing burrs was important because
loose aluminium swarf inside the tank could block the fuel filter, damage the fuel pump, or
contaminate the injectors. The tank had to be cleaned thoroughly after drilling to remove swarf,
dust, and any remaining debris from inside the tank.

The filler neck was then fitted to the tank. This was one of the more difficult parts of the assembly
because the filler neck angle and position had to satisfy the rules while also fitting around the
available space on the side of the car. The position was affected by the safety switch on the side
of Bruce, which limited where the filler could be placed. Because of this, the filler neck had to be
positioned carefully so that it remained accessible for filling and inspection without interfering
with the safety switch, bodywork, or nearby chassis members.

The drain plug was positioned at the lowest practical point of the tank so that fuel could be
removed safely during maintenance, storage, or inspection. The drain fitting had to be sealed
properly because it creates a potential leak point at the bottom of the tank. A suitable fuel
resistant sealing washer or sealant should be used, and the drain plug should be checked during
every leak test and after each time the tank is drained.

The fuel hoses were cut to length using a Stanley knife. Each hose length was measured on the
car before cutting so that the hose would be long enough to allow safe routing without being
excessively long. The hoses were cut cleanly and squarely so that they could seat properly on
the fittings. Poorly cut hose ends could create sealing problems or make the hose more likely to
slip off under pressure. After cutting, the hoses were fitted to the fuel pump, filter, regulator,
fuel rail, breather, and tank connections as required.

During assembly, the fuel lines were routed away from hot components such as the exhaust and
away from moving parts, sharp edges, and areas where the hose could rub against the chassis.
Where required, the hoses were secured using clamps or supports so that vibration would not
cause movement, rubbing, or fatigue. The hose clamps were tightened securely, but not so tightly
that they damaged the hose. Any hose that showed signs of cracking, flattening, poor fitment, or
fuel incompatibility would need to be replaced before the system could be used.

The fuel filter, pump, pressure regulator, and fuel rail connections were then assembled into the
system. The fuel filter was placed where it could protect the injectors from any remaining debris
while still being accessible for inspection and replacement. The pressure regulator was fitted so
that the fuel pressure could be controlled before the fuel reached the injectors. All fittings were
checked to make sure that they were tight, correctly seated, and compatible with petrol.

Before the tank was filled with petrol, the full assembly was inspected. The welds, filler neck,
cap, drain plug, breather, sight tube, fuel outlet, pump connection, filter, regulator, fuel rail, and
hose joints were all checked. The tank was then ready for the filling, draining, and leak checking
procedure. The fuel system should not be considered complete until it has passed a static leak
test, a pump-primed pressure test, and a final inspection after the first engine run.

## Filling, Draining, and Leak Checking Procedure

Once the fuel system has been manufactured and assembled, it must be checked carefully before
the engine is started. The purpose of this procedure is to make sure that the aluminium fuel
tank, filler neck, cap, sight tube, drain plug, pump, pressure regulator, filter, fuel rail, fuel lines,
and fittings are installed correctly and do not leak. Since the fuel system contains petrol, all
testing must be carried out carefully, gradually, and in a well-ventilated area away from sparks,
hot surfaces, naked flames, and electrical hazards.

Before any fuel is added, a full dry inspection should be carried out. The tank should be checked
for visible cracks, damaged welds, sharp edges, loose mounting points, and any signs of poor
sealing around fittings. The filler neck, filler cap, sight tube, breather line, drain plug, fuel pump
connection, fuel filter, regulator, and fuel rail connections should all be inspected. All fuel hoses
should be checked to make sure they are petrol-compatible, correctly clamped, and routed away
from the exhaust, moving parts, sharp chassis edges, and areas where they could rub during
vehicle vibration. The drain plug should also be checked to confirm that it is fully tightened and
sealed using a suitable fuel-resistant sealing method.

### Initial Filling Procedure

The car should be positioned on a level surface before filling so that the fuel level in the tank
and sight tube can be read correctly. A fire extinguisher and spill kit should be available nearby,
and only the minimum required amount of fuel should be added for the first test. The engine
should be cold, the master switch should be off, and all unnecessary electrical systems should
remain isolated during the initial filling stage.

Fuel should first be added slowly through the filler neck in a small quantity, for example
approximately 0.5 L. After this first amount has been added, the tank, filler neck, sight tube,
drain plug, pump fitting, breather connection, and lower fuel line connections should be inspected
for any signs of leakage. If no leaks are found, the fuel level can be increased gradually in small
steps. After each step, the system should be inspected again before more fuel is added. This
staged filling method reduces the amount of fuel that could escape if a leak is found.

The sight tube should be checked during filling to confirm that it gives a clear and useful indication
of the fuel level. The filler cap should then be installed and checked to make sure it seals securely.
The breather system should also be inspected to make sure it is not blocked and that the breather
line is routed safely away from hot components and the driver area. The tank should not be
overfilled, as space must be left for fuel movement and thermal expansion.

### Pump Priming and Pressure Leak Check

After the static filling check has been completed, the next stage is to check the pressurised side of
the fuel system. The pump should be powered only long enough to prime the system and build
fuel pressure. The engine should not be started during this first pressure check. While the pump
is priming, all pressurised connections should be inspected, including the pump outlet, fuel filter,
regulator, fuel rail, injector area, and all hose fittings.

If a fuel pressure gauge is fitted, the pressure should be checked to confirm that the regulator is
maintaining the expected fuel pressure. Any sudden pressure drop after the pump stops may
indicate a leak, poor sealing, or a fault in the regulator or pump assembly. The system should be
left pressurised for a short period and inspected again for fuel smell, wet fittings, dripping, or
staining around joints.

If any leak is found, the pump must be switched off immediately and the electrical system isolated.
The leaked fuel should be cleaned using suitable absorbent material, and the area should be
allowed to ventilate before any further work is carried out. The leaking joint should then be
repaired, tightened, resealed, or replaced before the pressure test is repeated. The engine should
not be started until the system can hold pressure without any visible leakage.

### Draining Procedure

The fuel system must be drained safely before major maintenance, tank removal, welding near
the car, long-term storage, or any work that could create sparks or heat. The car should be
placed on a level surface in a well-ventilated area, away from ignition sources. The engine should
be cold, the electrical system should be isolated, and a suitable approved fuel container should
be placed below the drain point.

The filler cap may be loosened carefully to allow air into the tank while draining, unless this
creates a spill risk. The drain plug should then be opened slowly so that the fuel flows in a
controlled way into the container. The fuel should never be drained onto the floor or into an
unsuitable container. Once the main fuel volume has drained, the car may be left for a short
period to allow remaining fuel to collect at the lowest point of the tank.

After draining, the plug and sealing washer should be inspected before being refitted. If the
washer is damaged, compressed, or not fuel-resistant, it should be replaced. The drain plug
should then be tightened correctly and checked during the next filling test. Any fuel removed
from the car should be stored or disposed of safely according to workshop procedures.

### Final Leak Checking Procedure

The final leak check should be carried out after the fuel system has been fully assembled and
before the car is driven. This check should include both a static inspection and a pump-primed
pressure inspection. The tank should be filled to the intended test level, the cap installed, and
the car left stationary while all joints are inspected. Particular attention should be given to the
filler neck, cap seal, sight tube connections, drain plug, pump outlet, filter, regulator, fuel rail,
injector seals, and all hose fittings.

The fuel pump should then be primed several times without starting the engine. After each
prime cycle, the system should be inspected for wetness, fuel smell, or pressure loss. A clean
paper towel may be used around fittings to help identify small leaks, but care must be taken not
to leave any material near hot or moving parts. The underside of the tank and the area below
the car should also be checked for drops of fuel.

Once the static and pressure leak checks have passed, the engine may be started for a short idle
test. During this test, one team member should monitor the fuel system while another is ready to
shut down the car immediately if a leak, strong fuel smell, unstable pressure, or abnormal engine
behaviour is observed. After the engine is switched off, the fuel system should be inspected again
because some leaks may only appear after vibration, heat, or pressure cycling.

The fuel system should be considered to have passed the leak check only if there are no visible
leaks, no fuel smell, no pressure loss, no rubbing hoses, no loose fittings, and no fuel collecting
under the car. Any failed check should be treated as a safety-critical issue and corrected before
further engine running or vehicle testing.
